Question 4 of 8
How do you reject that heat today?

Pick the cooling cascade that matches your plant. This decides how much of the year is free, how much runs on water, and how much lands on a compressor.

Where are the adiabatic pads installed? The dispatcher only uses the hardware you actually have:

Chiller condensers The common retrofit — fog on the air-cooled chillers. Assists the compressors on hot hours; cannot extend free cooling.
Dry coolers Wet media on the heat-rejection coils — extends free cooling toward wet-bulb, so cool nights run compressors-off and charge into the opened room.
Both The full toolkit: night wet free-cooling digs charging room, condenser fog defends the hot afternoons. Recommended for new installs.

Adiabatic systems can be dispatched for three different goals — choose what IceBrick should optimise on this site:

Power optimisation Dynamic setpoint: no fixed spray trigger — the pads are dispatched hour by hour, together with the ice, wherever they buy the most peak and PUE. Annual water is held at or below what your plant's own fixed trigger spends today.
Night-fogging The pads run at night on the dry coolers to their full depth, opening compressors-off charging room, and by day on the condensers. The only water rule is a cap on the change in site WUE: L/kWh added over the plant's water today.
Water optimisation Pads stay dry: the ceiling is pinned at today's peak, every spare megawatt beneath it charges ice, and the banked ice carries the hot hours. The pads spray only where even a full discharge cannot hold the line.
Question 5 of 8
Is your data hall air-cooled or liquid-cooled?

This decides what the next question even means. A liquid hall has a CDU between your facility loop and the chips, and the temperature that matters is the one the cold plates are promised. An air hall has chilled water going straight to coils, and the temperature that matters is what leaves the plant. Pick one and we will draw your loop.

Question 6 of 8
What water do your racks actually get?

Your IT does not see facility water — it sees whatever comes out of the CDU, one heat exchanger later. So we ask for the number your racks are promised and the step the CDU adds — the SLA ceiling comes on the next page, where IceBrick makes it usable. Facility water is then whatever the plant has to make to deliver it, and that is the loop the entire before analysis runs on.

Your year, before and after

Same axes, same scale. The red line is what the grid sees. Watch the spikes disappear — and notice that the flat line sits barely above the old baseline, because the energy was moved, not added. Blue is that move: load taken off the day and put back at night. Appendix — heat-wave reserve: storage beyond one night

Every number in this report is for a storage sized to one full night of charging — it starts and ends each day empty, and that is enough to hold the design day. A deeper array banks ice on a mild night ahead of a heat wave and spends it on days whose own nights are too warm to charge. Open the explorer to see how much extra peak each step of storage buys on this site — and where it stops paying.

Heat-wave reserve — how much extra storage is worth

Read the curve left to right: 1× is one night of charging, the size this report recommends. Each step to the right adds storage that carries ice across days. The line is the annual peak that size can hold; it flattens at the physical floor — the hour when IT, ancillary and pumps alone are the whole draw, which no amount of ice can go beneath. Extra storage is heat-wave insurance down to that floor, and nothing past it.
